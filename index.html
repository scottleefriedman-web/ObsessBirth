How it works

The OBsess Collaborative

All within a peer review environment, driven by education.

  1. 1

    Review 100% of deliveries

    Every delivery undergoes peer review, even when the outcome was ideal, to proactively measure physician and nurse adherence to targeted guidelines and best practices.

  2. 2

    Share each clinician’s own results

    Each physician and nurse sees their own adherence to the targeted best practices, alongside their peers’. When clinicians review their own data and compare it with their peers, they improve.

  3. 3

    Talk with the clinicians involved

    After a poor outcome, the clinicians involved are interviewed, so the team understands what really happened, in context.

  4. 4

    Fix systems together

    Poor outcomes are discussed at Monthly OB Multidisciplinary and Quarterly OB Leadership meetings, so system issues get addressed, not just individual cases.

Even when the outcome is perfect.

A good outcome doesn’t always mean every step went the way it should, and a hard outcome doesn’t always mean someone did something wrong. Reviewing every delivery against best practice, and sharing it with everyone, lets teams catch small drifts early and recognize the care that’s done right.

Low Apgar score: below 5 at 5 minutes after birth.

in a row without a newborn needing cooling therapy, during the same period.

Cooling therapy (induced hypothermia) is a neuroprotective treatment for newborns at risk of brain injury from suspected oxygen deprivation at birth.

What is an Apgar score?

Apgar scores are the gold standard for measuring newborn well-being. Since the 1960s, every newborn has been scored at 1 and 5 minutes after birth on five signs: skin color, heart rate, reflexes, muscle tone and breathing. Each sign scores 0 to 2, for a perfect total of 10. Low scores are associated with a higher risk of neurologic compromise, which is why reducing them matters so much.

For hospital and health-system leaders

Safer births start with leadership.

Advance quality. Lead in safety.

Department chairs, CMOs, CNOs, and quality and patient-safety leaders: your front-line teams do the work of learning from every case, but lasting change depends on leaders who understand the program, commit to it, and make safety a visible priority.

  1. Understand

    Leaders learn how team-wide, fair peer review works and why it matters to the people at the bedside.

  2. Commit

    Protected time for reviews, visible support from the top, and a clear message that learning, not blame, is the goal.

  3. Sustain

    Follow through on what the team learns, track outcomes over time, and keep the work going after launch.
